# Develop useful creative directions

Start from the audience's situation and the communication job. Choose a mechanism because it fits; do not cycle every assignment through all patterns.

| Pattern | Use when | Visual direction | Reject when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recognizable situation | The audience has a specific familiar problem | Show the relevant moment, object or task | It relies on an unsupported stereotype |
| Demonstration | Approved evidence can show how something works | Use an actual product view, screenshot or process detail | The image would fabricate proof |
| Revealing contrast | A meaningful comparison is factually supported | Contrast two states, choices or details | It invents a before/after result |
| Useful discovery | The message teaches something worth noticing | Point to the detail and make the lesson concrete | It hides a trivial answer behind exaggerated mystery |
| Objection or misconception | A real known objection blocks understanding | Pair the objection with a truthful explanation | It invents customer research or overstates the answer |
| Occasion or identity | A known occasion or shared activity is relevant | Show a recognizable context and its value | The scene is generic or excludes the real audience |
| Product or proof detail | Supplied evidence is a strong differentiator | Let the actual detail carry the message | It needs invented testimonials, badges or metrics |

## Candidate selection

For an open request, produce up to three different mechanisms, not three adjectives or colourways.
Include a specific headline and visual for each. Select one based on audience relevance, understandable message, credible payoff and feasible visual execution.
Do not invent numeric probabilities or a universal score threshold.
A clear brief may need only one direction. A resize needs none.

Keep copy concise for the intended use without inventing a character limit.
Specify reading order and the image's role; let production choose exact typography, geometry and tooling.
Real evidence often communicates more than decorative imagery.
Preserve exact logos, screenshots and approved words as source assets or editable layers when production can.

## Fictional worked example: educational local marketing

Supplied: a fictional agency offers a public checklist about checking business contact details.
Audience: independent shop owners. Goal: useful checklist visits. No testimonials or performance results.

1. **Customer situation:** “Can customers find the right number?” Visual: a phone beside a clearly illustrative business contact card, with the phone field as the focal point. Payoff: the caption gives the checking procedure.
2. **Useful discovery:** “Check these three contact details.” Visual: three plainly labelled fields: phone, opening hours and address. Payoff: the checklist explains the steps.
3. **Objection:** “Your contact details deserve a quick check.” Visual: owner reviewing their own listing, with no invented error message or claimed lost revenue.

Recommend direction 2 when immediate utility is the strongest reason to act. It explains the content without needing fear or a fabricated loss statistic.
These are proposed fictional concepts, not observed results. Use an actual approved screenshot if presenting evidence; never disguise a mockup as a customer's real listing.
